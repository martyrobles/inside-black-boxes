# Machine Discretion — Philosophy

> Provisional. Claims here are working theses until locked in `STATUS.md`.  
> Last updated: 2026-07-25

---

## Core tension (working)

Under the current legal framework, **only humans** can exercise **independent professional judgment and discretion**. Adjudicating government officials also exercise judgment in connection with benefits.

In practice, the real work of that judgment is increasingly done by **generative AI**, while a human still “signs” — a **legal fiction of human judgment** (Interview Q02: answer B).

**Machine Discretion** names the collision between:

1. A legal and ethical order that assigns judgment and discretion to human professionals (and human adjudicators), and
2. An operational reality in which generative systems perform the substantive work of that judgment under a human signature.

The open philosophical and practical question is how this implicates **ethical standards** and **legal standards**.

---

## The competence paradox (working)

The project does **not** assume humans are the better judgers.

Working claims from the author (Q02):

- Humans are **deeply flawed**
- Humans **cannot contain much context**
- As LLMs gain **broader context windows**, they gain better access to information and knowledge
- Therefore they may make **better decisions or adjudications**

This produces the project’s sharper question:

> What happens when machines are better than humans, but the law requires humans to make those calls?

So the tension is not only “AI is doing work reserved for humans.” It is also:

**Legal authority stays with humans; epistemic competence may move to machines.**

That is a legitimacy problem, not only a compliance problem.

## Working distinctions (not yet settled)

| Term | Working sense | Still open |
|------|---------------|------------|
| Independent professional judgment | Legally and ethically assigned to humans | What “independence” means under a legal fiction of human signing |
| Discretion | Judgment under uncertainty, with accountability | Whether a machine can have it, simulate it, or only outperform on some metrics |
| Legal fiction of human judgment | Human signs; model does the real discretionary work | When fiction becomes fraud, negligence, or necessary transition |
| “Better” adjudication | Author links to broader context / information access | Better *at what*? accuracy, consistency, fairness, lawfulness, legitimacy? |
| Context | Information a decision-maker can hold and use | Whether more context equals better judgment, or different failure modes |
| Generative AI | Systems producing the analytical or decisional work product | Tool vs. shadow adjudicator vs. superior reasoner the law will not seat |

## Pressure points (collaborator notes)

- Larger context ≠ automatically better discretion. Judgment includes values, accountability, political legitimacy, and the right to be wrong in a human way — not only retrieval of more facts.
- If machines become “better” on accuracy but cannot hold legal responsibility, the law may be protecting a **responsibility structure**, not a **competence ranking**.
- Answer B (legal fiction) plus “machines better” can pull in opposite directions: expose the fiction as illegitimate, *or* treat the fiction as a bridge until law catches up. The project has not chosen.
