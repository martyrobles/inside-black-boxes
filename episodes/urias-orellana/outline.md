## Cold open (~0:30)

Unanimous Court: when a court of appeals reviews whether an asylum applicant suffered *persecution* under the INA, it applies **substantial-evidence review** to the Board of Immigration Appeals’ determination — including the agency’s application of the statutory persecution standard to undisputed facts. The government wins; the First Circuit is affirmed.

**Transition:** “This is a workflow test for *Inside Black Boxes*. The case is real; the urgency is not. But the standard-of-review question shows up in every asylum petition for review.”

---

## Stakes — what was at issue (~1:30)

- Salvadoran family entered without authorization in 2021; sought asylum in removal proceedings.
- Urias-Orellana testified credibly about a *sicario* targeting his family since 2016 — shootings, threats, assault.
- IJ and BIA denied asylum: credible testimony, but past threats were not “so menacing as to cause significant actual suffering or harm” under First Circuit precedent; future fear claim also failed.
- **Cert question:** Circuit split over whether § 1252(b)(4)(B) requires substantial-evidence review when the BIA decides if undisputed facts rise to *persecution* under § 1101(a)(42)(A) — or whether courts apply de novo review to that “mixed” determination.

**Transition:** “So the fight is not about the facts on the ground in El Salvador. It’s about how hard it is to reverse the agency on persecution in federal court.”

---

## The holding (~2:00)

- **Held:** INA requires **substantial-evidence** review of the agency’s persecution determination — both underlying facts and application of § 1101(a)(42)(A) to those facts.
- **Outcome:** Judgment affirmed, **9–0**, opinion by Justice Jackson (March 4, 2026).
- Statutory hook: § 1252(b)(4)(B) — administrative findings “conclusive unless any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.”
- **Elias-Zacarias lives:** 1992 decision already required this standard; IIRIRA codified it rather than displacing it.

**[VERIFY AGAINST PDF]** Quote for recording:

> “We hold that § 1252(b)(4)(B) requires courts to review the entirety of the agency’s conclusions—both the underlying factual findings and the application of the INA to those findings—for substantial evidence.”

---

## Majority reasoning — three beats (~4:00)

### Beat 1 — The circuit split and why it mattered

- Some circuits reviewed the whole persecution inquiry for substantial evidence; others split fact review from de novo application of law.
- Jackson resolves the split in the government’s favor: one deferential standard for the whole persecution determination.

### Beat 2 — Elias-Zacarias and IIRIRA

- Elias-Zacarias: reversal only if evidence was “so compelling that no reasonable factfinder could fail to find the requisite fear of persecution.”
- § 1252(b)(4)(B) “largely tracks” that language — Congress codified deference, did not expand judicial review.
- IIRIRA’s other limits on review (jurisdiction-stripping, “manifestly contrary to law” clauses) reinforce a deferential read.

### Beat 3 — Why “mixed question” does not mean de novo here

- Petitioners argued persecution is a mixed question of law and fact → de novo review.
- Court: the “forest” is that Congress meant **deference to the whole persecution determination**, including application of the INA standard to accepted facts.
- **Wilkinson** and **Guerrero-Lasprilla** address *whether courts may review at all* under § 1252(a) — not the § 1252(b)(4) standard of review. Different statutes, different questions.
- Court did **not** reach petitioners’ **Loper Bright** argument (footnote in opinion).

**Transition:** “For practitioners, the doctrinal headline is simple: persecution determinations stay in the substantial-evidence lane.”

## Practitioner checklist (~2:00)

1. **Petition for review framing** — Lead with why the record *compels* a contrary persecution finding; “clear error” or de novo framing for the mixed determination is wrong after this decision.
2. **Preserve pure legal questions** — SG conceded purely legal questions about the *appropriate persecution standard* may still get de novo review; this case involved application of an agreed standard to facts.
3. **Credibility findings** — Even where testimony is credited, IJ’s persecution analysis gets deference (ties to *Ming Dai* on credibility as factfinding).
4. **Circuit precedent** — Any lingering split authority should be updated; First Circuit’s “menacing threats” threshold was not challenged here.
5. **Client counseling** — Credible harm narrative alone may not suffice; focus appellate resources on record-compulsion arguments.
